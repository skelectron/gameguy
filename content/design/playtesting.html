Prototyping & playtesting

Prototype the question

Every prototype should answer one question: "Is wall-jumping fun in tight corridors?" or "Do players understand the build menu?". Throw-away code is fine. A BRUTAL single-file app (dotnet run proto.cs) with rectangles is a perfect prototyping tool.

Running a playtest

  1. Don't explain. Hand over the controller and say only what a real player would know.
  2. Watch, don't help. Where people get stuck is your most valuable data.
  3. Ask them to think aloud: "Say what you're thinking as you play."
  4. Take notes with timestamps: "02:10 tried to jump on the background tree".
  5. Interview afterwards: "What was the goal? What was most frustrating? When did you want to stop?"
LISTEN TO PROBLEMS, NOT SOLUTIONS

Players are excellent at noticing that something is wrong and often poor at suggesting how to fix it. "Add a double jump" usually means "this jump felt unfair". Look for the cause underneath.

Instrument your game

Log events such as deaths (with position), level times and quits using Brutal.Logging. Plot the death positions on the level map to build a heat map. Clusters show you where the difficulty spikes are.

Iterate

Test → note → fix the biggest problem → test again with new people. Fresh testers matter, because once someone has learned your game they can't un-learn it.

EXERCISE

Run a 3-person playtest of your Coin Dash levels. Record death positions, make a heat map, and fix the top two problems. Write a short before-and-after report.
