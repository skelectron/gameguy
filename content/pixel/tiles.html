Tiles & tilesets

Why tiles?

Tiles let you build huge worlds from a small set of images. They save memory, keep art consistent, and work directly as collision data: in Coin Dash, the same '#' character is both the picture and the wall.

Seamless tiles

  • The tile's edges must match on opposite sides: left with right, and top with bottom.
  • Avoid strong single features. A unique crack repeated every 16 px makes the grid obvious. Spread detail evenly, and make 2–3 variants to scatter randomly.
  • Test by tiling 4×4 copies. Most editors have a tile preview mode for this.

Tileset organisation

For a platformer ground tileset you need: top-left, top, top-right, left, centre, right, bottom-left, bottom and bottom-right pieces (a "9-slice"), plus inner corners and single-tile pillars. Autotiling chooses the right piece automatically by checking each tile's neighbours:

Coin Dash uses the smallest possible version of this idea: it draws a grass strip only when the tile above is not solid (if (!Solid(tx, ty - 1))).

Maps in data

  • Text maps (like Coin Dash) are perfect for prototyping.
  • Tiled (mapeditor.org) is a free, popular editor. It exports JSON, which you can load with System.Text.Json.
  • Keep visual layers (background, decoration) separate from the collision layer.
